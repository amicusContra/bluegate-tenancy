## WHAT THE LAW ACTUALLY SAYS

I pulled every statute. Here's what matters:

### Your Rights as a Father (MCL 722.1003 + 722.1004)

The AOP you signed at the hospital is **"the equivalent to an
adjudication of parentage."** It gives you **"all rights and
duties of a parent."** Your daughters have the **"identical
status, rights, and duties of a child born in lawful wedlock."**

You are not a visitor. You are not an accessory. You are their
legal father with the same rights as if you and Shayla were
married. The law says so.

### Shayla's "Initial Custody" (MCL 722.1006)

Here's what the AOP guide says — and what Shayla will try to use
against you:

> "The mother has initial custody of the minor child."

That sounds like she wins. But read the rest:

> "...without prejudice to the determination of either parent's
> custodial rights, until otherwise determined by the court."

And:

> "This grant of initial custody to the mother does not, by
> itself, affect the rights of either parent in a proceeding to
> seek a court order for custody or parenting time."

**Translation:** When unmarried parents separate, the mother gets
custody by default — UNTIL a court decides otherwise. That's all
it means. It's a placeholder. It doesn't mean she's the better
parent. It doesn't mean you have no rights. It doesn't mean she
can do whatever she wants. It means the law needed a default rule
and they picked the mother.

Your job is to get to court and ask the court to "otherwise
determine" custody. That's what we're doing.

### The Status Quo — Why It Protects You (MCL 722.27(1)(c))

This is the part that changes everything.

Michigan law has a concept called **"established custodial
environment"** (ECE). It means: if a child has been looking to a
parent for love, discipline, food, shelter, and comfort for a
meaningful period of time — that counts. And the court will
protect it.

**The law says:**

> "The court shall not... change the established custodial
> environment of a child unless there is presented clear and
> convincing evidence that it is in the best interest of the
> child."

The cases say:
- An ECE can exist in **both homes** (Foskett v Foskett)
- An ECE can exist **even without a custody order** (Berger)
- The purpose of this rule is to **"erect a barrier against
  removal of a child from an established custodial environment"**
  (Baker v Baker; Vodvarka v Grasmeyer)

**Here's the punch:** For nine years, you and Shayla co-parented
those girls. No custody order. No court involvement. You fed
them, bathed them, took them to school, did homework, put them
to bed. You were the stay-at-home parent. Shayla was the
breadwinner. When you split up, she still relied on you —
through the summer, she brought them to you, you took care of
them, you were still Dad.

That means an established custodial environment existed in BOTH
homes — yours and hers. The girls looked to BOTH of you.

### Who Bears the Burden

When Shayla filed for SOLE custody, she was asking the court to
destroy the arrangement she helped build. She was asking to remove
you from the ECE that she herself created by relying on you.

**That means SHE bears the burden.** She has to prove — not by a
little, but by **clear and convincing evidence** — that taking you
out of the picture is better for the girls.

She can't. Because it's not true.

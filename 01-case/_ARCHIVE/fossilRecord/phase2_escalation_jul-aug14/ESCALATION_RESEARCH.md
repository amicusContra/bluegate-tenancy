## I. RETROACTIVE CHILD SUPPORT — Strong, Straightforward

### The Law

**MCL 722.717(2)**: "A child support obligation is only retroactive
to the date that the paternity complaint was filed unless..."

**MCL 552.452**: Same language for support complaints generally.

**MCL 552.603(2)**: Once a support order exists, it cannot be
retroactively modified EXCEPT from the date a modification
petition is filed.

Every month from filing to hearing date accrues. If the hearing

## II. UNJUST ENRICHMENT — Difficult but Distinguishable

### The Problem: Michigan Precedent

Michigan courts are hostile to unjust enrichment between
unmarried cohabitants. The key cases:

**Featherston v. Steinhoff, 226 Mich App 584 (1997)**:
- "Those engaged in meretricious relationships do not enjoy
  property rights afforded a legally married couple."
- Courts will ONLY enforce agreements with proof of "additional
  independent consideration."
- "Services rendered during a meretricious relationship are
  presumably gratuitous."
- To overcome: must show plaintiff expected compensation at the
  time services were rendered AND defendant expected to pay.

**Carnes v. Sheldon, 109 Mich App 204 (1981)**:
- Express and implied-in-fact contracts between cohabitants are
  enforceable — but ONLY with additional independent
  consideration beyond the relationship itself.
- Courts will NOT allow quantum meruit (implied-in-law contracts)
  because it would "resurrect common-law marriage."

**DeSchane v. Klug, COA 360677 (2022)**:
- Most recent published opinion. Confirmed Featherston.
- "Quantum meruit and unjust enrichment rarely have a role to
  play in disputes between unmarried couples."
- Plaintiff who contributed money and labor to partner's homes
  could not recover — no evidence of expectation of compensation
  at the time services were rendered.
- "The timing of plaintiff's demand speaks volumes" — he only
  asked for compensation after the breakup.

### Why DaSean's Case Is Distinguishable

The failed cases involve cohabitants who contributed labor or
money during a relationship and then claimed compensation after
the breakup. DaSean's case has unique facts:

**1. TWO WEDDING CEREMONIES = EXPRESS AGREEMENT**

DaSean and Shayla had two wedding ceremonies:
- Local ceremony (~2022)
- Hawaii ceremony (~2022) — Kevin Mok and Heather Hannon
  signed as witnesses

These ceremonies constitute an EXPRESS agreement to form a
marital partnership. The exchange of vows — "for richer or
poorer, in sickness and health" — IS the agreement. The
"additional independent consideration" is the mutual promise
to form a life partnership, witnessed by third parties and
publicly celebrated.

The Featherston court said: "This Court will, however, enforce
an agreement made during the relationship upon proof of
additional independent consideration."

Two ceremonies with witnesses IS additional independent
consideration. It's not a vague cohabitation — it's a
public declaration of partnership, twice.

**2. DELIBERATE NON-FILING BY A FINANCIAL PROFESSIONAL**

Shayla — a bank employee with professional expertise in
financial instruments, credit, and debt — CHOSE not to file the
marriage paperwork. Her stated reason: DaSean's military debts.

This is not a couple who casually forgot to get a marriage
license. This is a financial professional making a calculated
decision to maintain the APPEARANCE of marriage (ceremonies,
shared name, social presentation) while retaining the LEGAL
protections of non-marriage (no property division, no support
obligation, no retirement split, no financial disclosure).

She engineered the arrangement. He relied on it.

**3. DETRIMENTAL RELIANCE (Promissory Estoppel)**

Elements under Michigan law:
- A promise (two ceremonies, shared name, "planned to get
  legally married this year")
- Reasonable reliance (DaSean gave up career opportunities,
  became stay-at-home parent, had no independent financial
  identity)
- Detrimental change in position (9 years later: no credit,
  no savings, no car, no employment history, no retirement,
  overdrawn bank account)
- Injustice if promise not enforced (she walks away with 401k,
  career, credit history; he walks away with nothing)

**Key distinction from failed cases**: DaSean didn't contribute
money to her assets (the DeSchane pattern). He contributed
LABOR — 9 years of full-time caregiving — that ENABLED her
to accumulate assets. Her 401(k) contributions, her career
advancement, her uninterrupted employment — all were possible
because DaSean raised the children full-time.

**4. TIMING ARGUMENT**

The DeSchane court emphasized that the plaintiff only demanded
compensation AFTER the relationship ended, suggesting no
expectation of payment during it. DaSean's counter-argument:

He DIDN'T demand compensation because he believed they were
married (or would be). The non-demand isn't evidence of
gratuitous service — it's evidence of reliance on the
promised marriage. You don't demand payment from your spouse
for raising your shared children. He didn't demand it because
he thought he was a husband. She knew he wasn't — and she
engineered that.

### Viable Legal Theories

**Theory 1: Express Contract (strongest)**
- Two ceremonies = express agreement
- Terms: mutual support, shared responsibilities, partnership
- She breached by refusing to formalize (filing paperwork)
- Damages: value of 9 years of caregiving services ($210K-$312K)

**Theory 2: Promissory Estoppel**
- Promise to marry (ceremonies, shared name, 2026 plans)
- Reliance (gave up career, became stay-at-home parent)
- Detriment (no financial identity after 9 years)
- Must be enforced to prevent injustice

**Theory 3: Constructive Trust / Accounting**
- Her 401(k) and retirement assets accumulated during the
  period of his caregiving
- Equity demands an accounting of benefits received
- DaSean seeks a constructive trust over the portion of her
  retirement attributable to the caregiving period

### Honest Assessment

| Theory | Strength | Risk |
|--------|----------|------|
| Express contract (two ceremonies) | **Moderate-Strong** — unique facts distinguish from Featherston | Court may still find ceremonies aren't "additional independent consideration" |
| Promissory estoppel | **Moderate** — strong facts but Michigan courts are cautious | Statute of Frauds defense (marriage promises may need to be in writing under MCL 566.132) |
| Constructive trust | **Moderate** — equitable argument | Requires proving she was unjustly enriched, which circles back to Featherston |
| Straight unjust enrichment | **Weak** — Michigan law presumes gratuitous services | Would likely fail under current precedent |

### The Unjust Enrichment Timing Decision

**Option A — File now (aggressive)**:
Two active cases against her simultaneously. Increases pressure.
But may appear vindictive to the custody judge if raised.

**Option B — Signal through counsel (recommended)**:
Don't file. Have an attorney (or Kevin) communicate to Shayla
or her attorney that the claim exists and is calculated. Let
the threat of $210K-$312K motivate settlement on custody.

**Option C — File post-custody (conservative)**:
Wait until custody is resolved. Use custody discovery (income,
retirement, employment records) as the evidentiary foundation.
File the civil action separately. No risk of contaminating the
custody case.

**Recommendation**: Option B now, Option C later if she doesn't
settle. The claim is more powerful as a threat than as a filed
action — because once filed, she has to deal with it. Before
filing, she has to IMAGINE dealing with it, which is worse.

The Malpractice Standard

17. Defendant Banks held himself out as an attorney — "I'm a
    lawyer" — and then acted as one: evaluating a legal document,
    rendering a legal opinion on its sufficiency, and imposing
    consequences (denial of access) based on that opinion. By
    claiming the station of a lawyer, Banks invited evaluation
    of his conduct against the professional standard of that
    station.

18. Had Banks been a licensed attorney, his conduct on August 29,
    2026 would constitute malpractice:

    (a) He had a conflict of interest — he is the founder and
        administrator of the school where the children are
        enrolled, and the enrolling parent (Shayla Smith) is
        on one side of the custody dispute. He cannot provide
        disinterested legal evaluation of a document from the
        opposing parent;

    (b) He failed to read the document competently — a
        ten-second review of a parental authorization
        referencing an active custody case is not a competent
        legal evaluation;

    (c) He failed to contact the authorizing party — a
        competent attorney would have called DaSean Mitchell
        to verify the authorization, especially given the
        reference to active litigation;

    (d) He denied access based on the form of the document
        rather than its substance — without reading it
        thoroughly enough to determine whether it was valid;

    (e) He rendered a legal opinion without any legal authority
        to do so, from a position of institutional power
        (school administrator), to a person who had no reason
        to doubt his claimed credentials.

19. Because Banks claimed to be a lawyer and acted as one, the
    damages flowing from his conduct are properly measured
    against a malpractice standard — the standard of the station
    he claimed. Banks does not get the benefit of claiming legal
    authority without the corresponding accountability.

MOTION FOR MARRIAGE BY ESTOPPEL AND
EQUITABLE DIVISION OF MARITAL-EQUIVALENT ASSETS

JURY TRIAL DEMANDED

    Plaintiff DaSean Eddie Mitchell, appearing pro se, moves
this Court for an order recognizing the parties' nine-year
relationship as a marriage by estoppel or, in the alternative,
a putative marriage entitling Plaintiff to equitable division
of assets accumulated during the relationship, and states as
follows:

INTRODUCTION

1. Defendant Shayla Smith and Plaintiff lived together for
   approximately nine years in a committed relationship. They
   held two formal commitment ceremonies. Defendant went by
   Plaintiff's surname. They had two children together. They
   presented themselves to the world as a married couple.

2. Defendant's own counterclaim in this proceeding, however,
   makes clear that she considers these ceremonies and this
   relationship to NOT constitute a legal marriage. She has
   reverted to her maiden name. She has treated the
   relationship as something she can walk away from without
   any obligation to divide the assets that both parties built.

3. Defendant cannot have it both ways. She cannot hold herself
   out as Plaintiff's wife for nine years — extracting his
   labor, his wages, his childcare, and his earning capacity —
   and then declare the relationship was never a marriage when
   it is time to divide what she accumulated.

4. This is marriage by estoppel. Defendant induced Plaintiff's
   belief in a marriage-equivalent commitment, received the full
   benefits of that commitment, and should be estopped from
   denying it now.

LEGAL STANDARD

5. Michigan abolished common law marriage by statute in 1957.
   MCL 551.2. However, Michigan courts have long recognized
   that equitable principles prevent a party from denying the
   existence of a marriage-like relationship when they have
   held themselves out as married, induced reliance, and
   benefited from the arrangement.

6. In Carnes v. Sheldon, 109 Mich App 204 (1981), the Michigan
   Court of Appeals recognized that unmarried cohabitants may
   have enforceable agreements — express or implied — regarding
   the accumulation and division of property. The court held
   that equity will not allow one party to unjustly enrich
   themselves at the expense of the other.

7. In Featherston v. Steinhoff, 226 Mich App 584 (1997), the
   court recognized theories of implied contract, unjust
   enrichment, and constructive trust as available remedies
   for unmarried cohabitants who contributed to asset
   accumulation.

8. The doctrine of equitable estoppel prevents a party from
   denying a state of facts they previously induced another to
   believe and act upon. Lothian v. City of Detroit, 414 Mich
   160, 168 (1982). Where a party holds themselves out as
   married, induces their partner to contribute labor, income,
   and childcare in reliance on that representation, and then
   denies the marriage to avoid equitable division — estoppel
   applies.

FACTUAL BASIS

I. THE COMMITMENT CEREMONIES

9. The parties held TWO formal commitment ceremonies during
   their nine-year relationship:

   (a) CEREMONY ONE (approximately 2022): Held in Hawaii.
       Attended by Kevin Mok and Heather Hannon, who traveled
       from Michigan specifically to be present. The ceremony
       was treated by all participants as a marriage
       celebration.

   (b) CEREMONY TWO: Heather Hannon served as a witness.
       The ceremony was formal, witnessed, and intentional —
       not casual or ambiguous.

10. These were not informal exchanges of sentiment. They were
    planned events. People traveled. Witnesses were present.
    Vows were exchanged. The intent was clear: the parties were
    committing to each other as life partners.

II. HOLDING OUT AS MARRIED

11. Throughout the nine-year relationship, the parties presented
    themselves to the world as a committed couple — functionally
    equivalent to a married couple:

    (a) Defendant went by Plaintiff's surname during portions
        of the relationship;

    (b) The children bear Plaintiff's surname: Sahana Saige
        MITCHELL and Allanna Star MITCHELL;

    (c) The parties attended social events as a couple,
        including Kevin Mok's wedding in 2024, where the
        minor children served as flower girls;

    (d) The parties shared a household, shared finances, shared
        parenting responsibilities, and presented a unified
        family to friends, family, and community;

    (e) Plaintiff's family — including his mother, Deanna
        Mitchell — treated Defendant as a daughter-in-law and
        provided years of childcare and support to the
        "family" Defendant was part of;

    (f) Kevin Mok and Heather Hannon treated the parties as a
        married couple — traveling to Hawaii for a ceremony,
        having the children as flower girls, providing housing
        and support to the family unit.

12. No one in the parties' social circle treated this
    relationship as anything other than a marriage. The
    ceremonies, the shared name, the children, the family
    integration — every signal Defendant sent to the world
    said: this is my husband.

III. DEFENDANT'S POST-RELATIONSHIP CONDUCT REVEALS THE STRATEGY

13. After the relationship ended, Defendant:

    (a) Reverted to her maiden name (Smith), discarding
        Plaintiff's surname;

    (b) Filed a custody action — NOT a divorce action — thereby
        treating the relationship as if it never reached the
        level of a legal commitment;

    (c) Made clear through her filings that she considers the
        two ceremonies as non-binding — convenient for the
        romantic phase, disposable for the legal phase;

    (d) Used Kevin Mok and Heather Hannon — the same people
        who witnessed the commitment ceremonies — for personal
        benefit after the breakup (Cinderella tickets),
        maintaining the family friendships when useful while
        denying the relationship those friendships were built
        around;

    (e) Walked away with 100% of the assets accumulated during
        nine years of shared labor — her 401(k), her HSA, her
        credit history, her career advancement — while leaving
        Plaintiff with nothing.

14. Defendant's position is: the ceremonies were real enough to
    get nine years of Plaintiff's labor, but not real enough to
    require dividing the assets that labor helped create. This
    is the precise inequity that estoppel prevents.

IV. WHAT PLAINTIFF CONTRIBUTED AND WHAT DEFENDANT KEPT

15. During the nine-year relationship, Plaintiff contributed:

    (a) PRIMARY CHILDCARE: Plaintiff was the day-to-day
        caregiver for both children. He woke them, fed them,
        bathed them, took them to school, and was present when
        they came home. Defendant herself described herself as
        the "breadwinner" — acknowledging that Plaintiff's role
        was the domestic foundation of the household.

    (b) WAGES: Plaintiff worked at Workbox, Vascor, Refined,
        Expedient, and Uber over nine years. All earnings
        flowed into a household economy controlled by Defendant.

    (c) HOUSEHOLD LABOR: Plaintiff maintained the home,
        managed daily logistics, and provided the domestic
        infrastructure that enabled Defendant's career.

    (d) CAREER ENABLEMENT: Defendant's position at Lake Trust
        Credit Union — her salary, her 401(k), her HSA, her
        professional advancement — was only possible because
        Plaintiff provided free full-time childcare. Without
        Plaintiff, Defendant would have paid $15,000-$25,000
        per year for professional daycare, or reduced her hours,
        or left the workforce.

16. At the end of nine years, Defendant retained:

    (a) Her 401(k) retirement account (accumulated during the
        9 years Plaintiff provided childcare);
    (b) Her Health Savings Account (HSA);
    (c) Her salary and career trajectory at Lake Trust CU;
    (d) Her credit history (built during the relationship);
    (e) Access to at least nine different payment cards
        (identified in her own court exhibits);
    (f) Her share of the Smith household equity at 17370
        Dresden (mortgage through her employer, Lake Trust CU);
    (g) Control of all household documents, including
        Plaintiff's DD-214, SSN card, and birth certificate.

17. At the end of nine years, Plaintiff retained:

    (a) No savings;
    (b) No credit;
    (c) No retirement;
    (d) No property;
    (e) Two collections on his credit report — both from
        Defendant's obligations (her car insurance, her
        wedding debt);
    (f) No access to his own identity documents.

18. In a divorce, the 401(k), HSA, and career-related assets
    accumulated during the marriage would be subject to
    equitable division under MCL 552.19 and MCL 552.401.
    Defendant's position — that no marriage existed — allows
    her to keep 100% of assets that were built on 50% (or more)
    of Plaintiff's labor.

V. THE PUTATIVE SPOUSE DOCTRINE

19. In the alternative, if this Court does not apply marriage by
    estoppel, Plaintiff asserts the putative spouse doctrine.
    A putative spouse is one who believes in good faith that a
    valid marriage exists. Plaintiff participated in two
    commitment ceremonies, believed they constituted a binding
    commitment, and relied on that belief for nine years.

20. The putative spouse doctrine protects the innocent party who
    relied in good faith on the existence of a marriage. It
    would be fundamentally inequitable to allow Defendant —
    who organized the ceremonies, accepted the commitment, went
    by Plaintiff's name, and extracted nine years of labor —
    to now claim the relationship was legally meaningless
    because no marriage license was filed.

21. If a license was the only missing element, the question is:
    whose responsibility was it? Defendant controlled the
    household finances, the household documents, and the
    household's legal affairs. If the ceremonies were intended
    to be legally binding, Defendant — the banking professional
    with PATRIOT Act compliance training, who understands legal
    documentation — was the party best positioned to ensure a
    license was obtained. Her failure to do so, followed by her
    claim that no marriage exists, is itself evidence that she
    structured the relationship to extract Plaintiff's labor
    without the legal obligations of a spouse.

VI. THE CINDERELLA TICKETS — SELECTIVE USE OF FAMILY TIES

22. After the parties' relationship ended, Defendant used Kevin
    Mok and Heather Hannon — the same friends who traveled to
    Hawaii for the first commitment ceremony, who served as
    witnesses, whose wedding the parties attended together —
    for Cinderella tickets for the minor children.

23. Defendant maintained these family friendships when they were
    useful to her. She accepted the benefits of the family
    network that the "marriage" created. She used the witnesses
    to her own commitment ceremony as a resource for her
    children's entertainment.

24. Defendant cannot selectively benefit from the marriage
    relationship — using the family, the friends, the network —
    while denying the marriage itself when it is time to divide
    the assets.
